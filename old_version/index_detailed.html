Weighted Bayesian Conformal Prediction

Xiayin Lou1  ·  Peng Luo2

1Technical University of Munich    2Massachusetts Institute of Technology

Paper arXiv (soon) Code (soon) BibTeX
TL;DR — Conformal risk control keeps its guarantee only under exchangeability or a covariate shift whose likelihood ratio is known exactly. WBCP handles the case that actually occurs at deployment — an estimated ratio — by deriving the exact Bayesian posterior over the deployed risk of every threshold, and backing it with a finite-sample certificate for what that threshold really incurs.

Abstract

Machine learning predictors are rarely deployed on data that match the data they were calibrated on. Conformal prediction and its risk-control generalization promise distribution-free guarantees at deployment, but only under exchangeability or a covariate shift whose likelihood ratio is known exactly—and the estimated-ratio case, the one that occurs in practice, is explicitly open, out of reach of the Bayesian construction available under exchangeability. We reach it from a different starting point: pushing a Dirichlet process prior on the calibration distribution through the likelihood ratio yields the exact Bayesian posterior over the deployed risk. That posterior is exact given the weight function, which at deployment is itself estimated, so we further prove finite-sample upper and lower bounds on the risk the selected threshold actually incurs, in terms of the weight-estimation error and the conditional shift. On synthetic and real covariate shifts, in regression and classification, the realized failure rate then stays close to target where shift-blind selection fails, and what deployment receives is a posterior over the risk of every threshold rather than a single number.

How it works

Place a Dirichlet-process prior on the calibration distribution and let its concentration go to zero: the posterior is the Bayesian bootstrap. Covariate shift is a deterministic change of measure, so pushing that posterior through the likelihood ratio gives the exact posterior over the deployed score distribution—the normalized weighted Bayesian bootstrap. Each posterior draw is one candidate deployed risk curve; the threshold is chosen where a β fraction of those curves stays below the target risk α.

Exact posterior

Scaled Dirichlet, not assumed Dirichlet

The induced mass law is a scaled Dirichlet—Dirichlet only at uniform weights, where the exchangeable BQ-CP posterior is recovered exactly. Kish’s effective sample size neff emerges from the derivation as the unique two-moment concentration, and exact sampling costs the same as Dirichlet sampling: O(n) per draw.

Data-conditional guarantee

Risk control given your calibration set

The posterior loss variable stochastically dominates the posterior risk under every prior on the state of nature, so the β-credible threshold controls the deployed risk at failure credibility 1 − β—conditional on the observed calibration set, not just on average over calibration draws.

Deployment certificate

Finite-sample, two-sided, computable

The realized risk deviates from α by at most Δw·Δs + Δshift + ηn: weight error hurts only where the score is non-pivotal (an either-arm, doubly-robust structure), genuine conditional shift is priced separately, and the entire cost of the Bayesian selection is one clamp mass computed at prediction time.

Explore the results

All rules are compared at the same specification: deployed risk at most α with failure rate at most 5%. The charts below are computed from the paper’s raw per-trial experiment outputs.

Which calibration sets exceed the target risk?

Distribution of the realized deployed risk over 10,000 independent calibration sets (synthetic heteroskedastic regression, n = 200, α = 0.1). Red mass to the right of the α line marks calibration sets whose deployed threshold violated the target.

Under shift, the shift-blind posteriors (BQ-CP, RCPS) concentrate past the target—more data makes them confidently wrong—while the expectation-only W-CRC rule violates in roughly half of the calibration sets, exactly what an expectation guarantee permits. WBCP’s failure rate sits at the nominal 5%. BQ-CP was not run in the exchangeable control; at uniform weights it coincides with WBCP by construction.

Failure rate and interval length as the shift grows

Shift strength γ tilts the test covariates toward the noisy end (γ = 0: no shift). Left: fraction of calibration sets whose realized risk exceeded α, against the 5% target failure rate. Right: the price of validity—mean deployed interval length. 10,000 trials per point, estimated weights.

No valid interval can be shorter than the oracle length under the tilted law; the blind rules’ short intervals are paid for by their violation rates. At the strongest shifts WBCP abstains on a small fraction of calibration sets (0.7% at γ = 1.5, 2.2% at γ = 2.0) rather than certify without support.

Across all four benchmarks

Percentage of calibration sets whose deployed threshold exceeded the target risk (the specification allows 5%). Synthetic regression and multilabel classification use estimated weights under constructed shifts; PovertyMap-WILDS is a natural country shift; Fashion-MNIST is an entropy tilt.

Knows when to abstain

neff ≈ 7 ⇒ no certificate

In the multilabel benchmark at n = 10, no threshold reaches 95% credibility in 93% of trials: WBCP abstains, and never violates when it does certify. The shift-blind rules certify regardless—and by n = 100 they violate in essentially every trial.

Attributable errors

Every deviation is accounted for

Oracle-weight reference runs separate the certificate’s terms: the distance from oracle to nominal is finite-sample concentration; the gap between estimated and oracle weights is the weight-estimation premium Δw—visible in isolation when the density-ratio model is deliberately mis-specified (28.0% vs. 6.5% on Fashion-MNIST).

What deployment receives

A posterior, not a single number

The output is a posterior over the risk of every threshold: a deployed threshold, its spread σpost, the effective sample size, and the computable clamp mass—each matched to a diagnostic available at prediction time, including the one term no unlabeled diagnostic can see.
