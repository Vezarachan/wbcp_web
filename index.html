Weighted Bayesian Conformal Prediction

Xiayin Lou1  ·  Peng Luo2

1Technical University of Munich    2Massachusetts Institute of Technology

Paper arXiv Code BibTeX

Conformal risk control promises “risk ≤ α” — but its Bayesian reading, and the failure-rate guarantee that comes with it, exists only when deployment looks exactly like calibration. Our observation: the posterior was never about ranks or spacings. It is a posterior on the calibration distribution itself, and covariate shift is just a change of measure. Pushing the same Bayesian-bootstrap masses through the likelihood ratio gives the exact posterior over the deployed risk — nothing approximate, at the same O(n) cost — and the certificate we prove tells you how far to trust it once the ratio itself is estimated.

Abstract

Machine learning predictors are rarely deployed on data that match the data they were calibrated on. Conformal prediction and its risk-control generalization promise distribution-free guarantees at deployment, but only under exchangeability or a covariate shift whose likelihood ratio is known exactly—and the estimated-ratio case, the one that occurs in practice, is explicitly open, out of reach of the Bayesian construction available under exchangeability. We reach it from a different starting point: pushing a Dirichlet process prior on the calibration distribution through the likelihood ratio yields the exact Bayesian posterior over the deployed risk. That posterior is exact given the weight function, which at deployment is itself estimated, so we further prove finite-sample upper and lower bounds on the risk the selected threshold actually incurs, in terms of the weight-estimation error and the conditional shift. On synthetic and real covariate shifts, in regression and classification, the realized failure rate then stays close to target where shift-blind selection fails, and what deployment receives is a posterior over the risk of every threshold rather than a single number.

From prior to threshold, in three steps

  1. Put a prior on the calibration distribution itself.

    A Dirichlet-process prior on the calibration law P, concentration τ → 0+, leaves the Bayesian bootstrap: the posterior lives on the observed points, each carrying a random mass

    P* = Σi Vi δ(Xi,Yi),    Vi = Ei / ΣkEk,    Ek ~ Exp(1) i.i.d.

    Every second, one fresh draw of the masses Vi — each draw is one plausible calibration distribution. Nothing beyond i.i.d. sampling enters here — and crucially, this posterior is about the distribution, not about ranks or quantile spacings, so covariate shift can act on it.

  2. Covariate shift is a change of measure — apply it to the posterior.

    The deployment law is a deterministic functional of the calibration law: tilt by the likelihood ratio, T(Q) = w·Q / ∫w dQ. Applying T to a posterior draw P* multiplies the mass of point i by wi and renormalizes — and the old normalizer ΣkEk cancels between numerator and denominator:

    Ṽi = Viwi ΣjVjwj  =  wiEi ΣjwjEj

    The same draw, before and after the tilt: mass moves toward the points the deployment distribution actually visits. The pushforward of an exact posterior is an exact posterior — no Dirichlet is assumed at any point. At uniform weights (w ≡ 1) the exchangeable theory returns unchanged, and Kish’s neff = (Σwi)2/Σwi2 falls out of the mass law’s moments rather than being imposed.

  3. Each draw is one deployed risk curve; select where β of them agree.

    A mass draw turns the calibration losses into one candidate deployed risk curve; its α-crossing is one plausible threshold. Repeating this is the whole sampler — and it is exactly what the animation above is doing: 

    for m = 1 … M:                                # M ≈ 1000 draws
    draw E₁, …, Eₙ, Eₙ₊₁ ~ Exp(1)             # one prior draw
    Ṽᵢ ∝ wᵢ Eᵢ,  test atom Ṽₙ₊₁ ∝ w̄ Eₙ₊₁      # push through the shift
    L(λ) = Σᵢ Ṽᵢ ℓ(λ; Xᵢ, Yᵢ) + Ṽₙ₊₁ Bℓ        # deployed risk curve
    λ⁽ᵐ⁾ = smallest λ with L(λ) ≤ α            # its α-crossing

λ_HPD = β-quantile of {λ⁽¹⁾, …, λ⁽ᴹ⁾}           # the HPD rule
deploy λ_dep = max(λ_wcp, λ_HPD)                # never below the W-CP quantile

    Left: each mass draw yields one deployed risk curve, crossing the target α at that draw’s threshold (ticks). Right: the crossings accumulate into the threshold posterior; λHPD is the point that β = 95% of draws declare safe. O(n) per draw — the exact posterior samples at the cost of the Dirichlet it replaces, and the clamp to the weighted conformal quantile λwcp means the classical marginal guarantee is inherited unchanged.

Nothing is assumed under shift. The Dirichlet of the i.i.d. theory is not postulated and then patched — it is derived (Bayesian bootstrap on the calibration law) and then pushed through the shift. What deployment adds — an estimated ratio, a possibly impure shift — is priced by a finite-sample certificate in the paper, with computable upper and lower bounds on the deployed risk.

Does it hold up at deployment?

Every rule is asked for the same thing: deployed risk at most α, failing in at most 5% of calibration sets. Bars show how often each rule’s threshold actually exceeded the target risk.

BQ-CP and RCPS select as if calibration matched deployment; W-CRC uses the weights but guarantees only an expectation over calibration sets.
